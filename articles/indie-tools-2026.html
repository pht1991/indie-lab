Top 7 Free & Paid Tools to Ship an Indie Game on a Budget

By Haitao Pan · Game Developer & Studio Owner · 10 min read

TL;DR: I build and ship original games on a near-zero budget — my current project runs on Cocos Creator 3.8 for the WeChat mini-game platform, and I pay for almost nothing. Below are the 7 tools I actually use to take a game from an empty folder to a published build. Six are free; exactly one is worth paying for. The whole point is to spend on time, not on features you'll never touch.

Disclosure: Some of the tool links below may become affiliate links (through PartnerStack / Awin) once those programs are connected — at no extra cost to you. Where a program isn't connected yet, links point to the tool's official page. I only list tools I've actually used to ship games.

1. The mindset: buy time, not features

The bottleneck for a solo or tiny team is never "do we have enough engine features?" It's how many hours you have before you burn out or run out of runway. A $0 engine plus a $0 art tool will get you 90% of the way to a shippable game. Money is best spent on the single point in your pipeline where a paid tool hands you back the most hours — not on asset packs you'll admire and never use.

This is the same principle I apply everywhere else in the stack. When I picked an engine I weighted package-size ceilings and iteration speed over raw feature lists (more on that in my engine comparison), and when I trimmed my build I cut dead weight rather than buying my way out (the teardown is in my bundle-size breakdown). The tool list below is the same instinct applied to your software budget.

2. The free workhorses (tools 1–5)

Five of the seven are completely free and cover the entire core pipeline: build, art, code, audio, publish.

1 · Cocos Creator 3.8 — engine (what I actually ship on)

Free, TypeScript-first, and it targets both WeChat mini-game and web from one project. I've shipped a full 750×1334 survival/crafting game on it — the architecture and the painful bits are in my Cocos mini-game post-mortem. For a solo dev it removes the "do I need a build server" question entirely.

Cocos Creator official page →

2 · Godot — the free fallback engine

Open-source, no strings, and GDScript is gentle if you're coming from a non-game codebase. I keep it in my back pocket for prototypes that don't fit Cocos's mini-game-first model. You don't need both — but knowing the second option exists stops you over-committing to one ecosystem.

Godot official page →

3 · Blender — art and animation, free

People sleep on Blender for 2D. You can mock up sprites, bake simple textures, and rig lightweight animations without touching a paid suite. It has a learning curve, but it's a curve you climb once and then own forever. For a stylized low-poly or flat-shaded look it's genuinely enough to ship.

Blender official page →

4 · Visual Studio Code — your code editor

Free, fast, and the extension ecosystem covers every language you'll touch in game dev. There is no reason to pay for an editor. The one tip that mattered for me: wire up the type-definitions for your engine so autocomplete catches API drift early instead of at runtime.

VS Code official page →

5 · Audacity + itch.io / GitHub Pages — audio and publishing, free

Audacity covers SFX cleanup and simple mixing. For distribution, itch.io is free to publish on and friendly to weird, experimental games; GitHub Pages (what this very site runs on) is free static hosting if you're shipping a web build. You can be live with zero hosting bill.

Audacity official page →

3. The one paid tool I'd never skip: Aseprite (~$20)

If your game is pixel-art — and a lot of solo breakout hits are — Aseprite is the one purchase I'd defend against any "stay 100% free" purist. The free alternatives technically exist, but the time tax of fighting their workflows (timeline jank, weak onion-skinning, no clean export pipeline) costs you far more than twenty bucks across a real project. Aseprite's timeline, layers, and sprite-sheet export are boring in the best way: they just work, every day.

That's the whole thesis of this article in one tool. I'm not paying for "more features." I'm paying for the hours I'd otherwise lose to a clumsy freebie. If your art isn't pixel-based, Blender covers you for free and you can skip this entirely — which is exactly why I refuse to call it mandatory for everyone.

4. What I'd avoid spending on early

The traps are all "pay now to feel productive later," and they quietly sink small budgets:

  1. Asset packs before you have a fun loop. A gorgeous environment pack doesn't make a vague prototype fun. Build the loop ugly first; buy art only when you know which screens actually ship. This is the same "pull one real lever" discipline I wrote about in my idle-loop design notes —素材 is a cosmetic lever, not a core one.
  2. Engine-plugin subscriptions. The monthly "pro" tier that promises to automate something you'll do twice. Most of those jobs are a weekend of code you own forever, versus a recurring fee you forget to cancel.
  3. All-in-one "game maker" bundles. They trade flexibility for convenience and you'll hit the wall exactly where your game is unique. A free engine + free art tools compose better than a paid walled garden for 99% of indie scopes.

5. How this fits my actual pipeline

None of these are theoretical for me. The engine and the bundle discipline are live in the game I'm shipping; the art and audio tools are what I reach for on every new screen. The order above is literally the order I'd rebuild from today: free engine, free art, one paid pixel tool, and assets only as a last resort. The constraint forces focus, and focus is the real scarce resource — not the software budget.
